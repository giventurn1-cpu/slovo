Why there is no speech recognition here

It would be easy to bolt a transcription model on and advertise fully automatic lyrics. We deliberately did not. Speech recognition is unreliable over a finished mix — it mangles stacked harmonies, ad-libs and anything sung fast — and correcting its guesses usually takes longer than pasting the words you already have. It also normally means uploading your audio to somebody else’s server, which is the one thing this tool never does. So the work is split where each side is strong: you know the words, the tool works out the timing.

Line mode or word mode

Line mode is the classic lyric video: a line appears, holds while it is sung, and gives way to the next. It is calmer and much easier to read on a phone. Word mode highlights each word as it lands, the way karaoke does — good when the delivery is fast or when the text is part of the rhythm rather than a caption to it. It is a single switch, and changing your mind costs nothing.

Typography that survives a small screen

Most lyric videos are watched at the size of a hand. That rules out thin weights, long unbroken lines and low-contrast colours over a busy scene. Font, weight, size and position are all adjustable, and the text can react to the track so it moves with the music instead of floating above it. In vertical formats the safe zones of TikTok, Reels and Shorts are marked out, so a line never ends up underneath the platform’s own buttons.

Burned in, not a subtitle file

The words are drawn into the video itself rather than shipped as a separate subtitle track. Feeds have no concept of a subtitle file, and even on YouTube a viewer has to switch captions on. Burning them in means the video looks the same everywhere, in the typeface you chose rather than the platform’s default.
