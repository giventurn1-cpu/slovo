Desktop and phone, honestly

On desktop Chrome or Edge the render runs faster than real time: a three-minute track finishes before it would have finished playing, and you can leave the tab in the background while it works. On a phone the same visuals appear and the same project opens, but the recording path is slower — roughly the length of the song — and the tab needs to stay in front. Nothing about the picture changes; only how the frames get written. If you have a computer to hand, build there and use the phone to watch and share.

What actually makes a visualizer look expensive

Three things, and none of them is the number of templates. The first is whether the motion agrees with the music — a scene that pulses on a steady timer reads as decoration, while one that jolts on the kick reads as part of the track. The second is resolution and bitrate: heavy gradients and particles band badly at low bitrates, which is why a 360p free tier looks cheap no matter how good the template was. The third is your own material in the frame — cover art, a real photograph, a logo — because a stock scene with no trace of you in it looks like every other stock scene.

Common mistakes worth avoiding

  • A static image stretched across four minutes. Feeds punish it and viewers scroll past. Give the frame a reason to move.
  • Text in the corner of a vertical video. That is exactly where the platform draws its own buttons. Keep titles inside the safe area.
  • Exporting one aspect ratio and cropping it later. Cropping 16:9 to 9:16 throws away the composition. Export each ratio from the same project instead.
  • No text at all. A large share of feed views happen muted; without words the track is effectively silent.
